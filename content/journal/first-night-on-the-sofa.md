A lot has happened since [my last entry](/journal/first-ten-days) on 9 October: Barnie's first full night in the living room with us, his first proper zoomies, and some real progress at the kitchen door. Here's everything from the last couple of weeks.

## Hygiene, a carpet reset, and losing the landing

The landing worked as Barnie's safe base for his first couple of weeks, but it came at a cost. Despite puppy pads and a liner, some wee had made it through to the carpet, and once that happens the usual answer is an enzyme cleaner.

So on 18 October we hit the reset button on the landing carpet with a full Bactador treatment. That meant moving Barnie downstairs, off the landing he'd made his own. After giving the cleaner the 48 hours it needs to work, the area was fully dried, aired with a fan and vacuumed. We've kept puppy pads in key spots indoors as a low-pressure safety net while he learns household toilet etiquette.

::callout{type="info"}
**A tip that caught us out:** if your dog's safe space is on carpet, buy a bigger liner than you think you need. Ours didn't cover enough of the area, and that's how the wee reached the carpet.
::

Losing the landing is what set off the nights outside, which I'll come to below.

## Breakthrough: a full night on the sofa with us

It's funny how things flip. For his first couple of weeks the problem was the opposite: Barnie lived on our landing, and we struggled to get him *out* of the house. Since the move downstairs, he's swung the other way and sticks strictly to his outdoor garden routines.

So it was a huge milestone when Barnie spent a 20-hour stretch indoors, including his first full night in the living room with us, asleep on the sofa!

![Barnie lying on a brown leather sofa, curled up on a grey cushion with his eyes half open](/images/journal/barnie-first-sofa-night/chilling-on-the-sofa.webp){width="1080" height="1440" style="max-width:24rem;margin-inline:auto;display:block"}

*Chilling on the sofa on 20 October, his first full night in the living room with us. (He's half awake!)*

**Passive processing:** he spent the evening calmly soaking in the sounds of the house, watching the TV, and drifting into deep sleep on the cushions.

**Communication:** in the morning he clearly told us he needed the toilet by circling near the back door. He went out to do his business, came straight back inside for breakfast, and then started his morning garden patrol.

## Threshold resistance and patrol loops

With morning energy surges, his street-smart survival instincts occasionally flare up. Barnie will sometimes fall back into "patrol mode": taking a treat at the kitchen threshold and immediately darting back into the garden so he doesn't feel trapped.

To help him bridge the gap without forcing or cornering him, we've refined our approach:

- **Deep food trails:** moving high-value treats (warm Chappie, hot dog slices, boiled carrots, and smelly dry sausage treats) 4 to 6 feet into the kitchen, so he has to commit his whole body well past the doorframe.
- **Smear and trail:** dropping casual, continuous scent lines across the patio into the house, so he crosses on autopilot.
- **Ghost protocol:** no eye contact, standing out of his line of sight, and letting him explore the threshold completely unwatched, to build genuine trust.

## Zoomies!

We've also seen his very first wild bursts of zoomies across the lawn! Seeing a rescue of around 18 months let go of his self-control and sprint full tilt felt like a real sign that he's starting to shake off some built-up stress.

## Nights outside: he prefers the bushes

That first full night on the sofa was a breakthrough, but it isn't every night yet. Since the move off the landing, he's spent five or six nights outside, and getting him back into the house is still the hardest part of the day.

When he stays out, he heads for his favourite sheltered spot under the bush by the fence. We leave the garden office open with the light on, so he always has somewhere big and dry with a roof (we don't leave the heaters on, as the door has to stay open), but for now he prefers the bushes.

*Out by the fence on the evening of 18 October, curled up next to the bushes.*

The crux of it all is thresholds. Whether it's the garden office or the house, he'll sometimes stop at the doorway and whine, almost as if he really wants to come in but survival instinct, or fear, stops him. It's hard to listen to, and it's why the [threshold work above](#threshold-resistance-and-patrol-loops) matters so much.

Food is the bridge. He'll come part way into the kitchen to eat, as long as we leave the room. Some evenings we get him all the way in with a treat trail and a lot of patience, then calmly close the back door behind him. And every so often, more often at weekends when everyone's home, he just walks in of his own accord, which is the best feeling.

It's tempting to go out and bring him in, but grabbing or cornering him would undo exactly the trust we're trying to build, and the house would become the place where things happen to him rather than the place he chooses. So the door stays open, the trails stay down, and we wait.

What reassures me is that he isn't pacing anxiously out there. He's making a calm, deliberate choice about where he feels secure. With the garden office light left softly glowing and zero pressure from us, every day adds another deposit to his "indoor trust bank".

## What about a harness?

A harness is on the plan, but it isn't the first priority yet. Barnie is terrified of anything going over his head, and I don't want to set him back. Right now the most important thing is getting him used to living indoors and with us. The harness will come once he's more settled.

**Next steps:** more low-pressure threshold trails during office hours, keeping indoor routines warm and inviting, and letting him set the pace as he realises the sofa is his permanent den.
