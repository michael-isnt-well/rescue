In [my last entry](/journal/curiosity-thresholds-and-fireworks) Barnie was exploring the kitchen and winning night-time threshold battles. Then, on 11 November, he hit a wall.

## The trigger stack

It wasn't one thing. First he had a fright on the upstairs landing. Then, not long after, the vacuum cleaner fell over near the back door. On their own, either might have been a blip. Stacked together, they flipped his survival switch. Overnight, the house went from somewhere he was starting to trust to an unpredictable ambush zone, and the back door became the scariest place of all.

## Two nights and nearly three days outside

It was a cold snap, and Barnie spent two freezing nights and nearly three full days in the garden. He flatly refused to cross the back door threshold, even for warm meals.

He had options. The garden office was open, and so was the summer house, lined with his familiar bedding. The summer house is old and not insulated, but it's dry and it's a roof over his head. He chose the bushes anyway. For a dog who started life on the street and is running on instinct, natural cover with escape routes in every direction still felt safer than any man-made structure with one way in and one way out.

Watching him choose frost and bushes over a warm house was hard, especially after the progress of the last few weeks. But every time we tried to coax or lure him, it only confirmed what he already suspected: doorways are traps.

## How we broke the freeze

**Calories before training.** In my last entry I wrote about only feeding him indoors, and at first we stuck to it: we let him skip a meal rather than feed him outside. But as the second night dropped below freezing, it stopped making sense. Calories count when a dog is sleeping out in the cold. So we put his meals (warm Chappie and soaked Forthglade cold-pressed) on the patio right next to the back door. Keeping him fed and warm came first while he calmed down. The door can wait; his body can't.

**Choices without expectations.** The summer house, his bedding and familiar smells stayed available, but we didn't push him towards any of them. If the bushes were where he felt safe, the bushes were fine.

**No more door-watching.** We stopped hovering, coaxing and making eye contact through the glass. Instead, during quiet spells, we opened the back door briefly with zero expectation and walked away.

## The breakthrough

Once the pressure was gone and the adrenaline had finally worn off, after nearly three days, Barnie walked up to the back door on his own and stepped inside. No treat trail, no prompt. He just came in.

## Giving him the landing back

He's only been back inside for a few hours as I write this, so it's early days. But we've made one big decision: he gets the upstairs landing back as his decompression zone. It was his very first safe base when he arrived, and even though his first fright happened up there, it's where he chose to go once he was in.

And within a few hours of coming in, this is where he was:

*Fast asleep on the landing on the evening of 13 November, a few hours after he walked back in.*

After nearly three days of sleeping with one eye open in the bushes, I don't blame him.

Two rules are in place from here:

- **Hands off until he initiates.** No reaching for him, no approaching him. He comes to us.
- **Clear paths downstairs.** We're clearing the ground floor of anything that could fall, clatter or startle him, the vacuum cleaner included.

## Remembering where he's come from

I'll be honest: this one was frustrating. After weeks of kitchen exploring and night-time wins, it felt like we were back at the start, and it's hard not to take that personally when you've been so careful.

But progress with a rescue dog isn't linear, and it helps to remember his past. Technically, Barnie started out as a street dog: he was one of an abandoned litter found behind a supermarket in Romania. But he's spent most of his life since then in a shelter, surrounded by lots of other dogs. He's had well under two months of living in a house, and none of that life prepared him for landings, back doors or vacuum cleaners. A falling vacuum cleaner is nothing to us. To him, it was proof that the house could still turn on him. Seen that way, three days in the bushes isn't him going backwards. It's him falling back on the instincts he was born with and the only world he's really known, until he was ready to trust us again.

::callout{type="info"}
**What I'd tell another adopter:** a setback like this doesn't wipe out the progress before it. A frightened dog needs to feel safe before they can learn anything, so when they're scared, feeding and shelter come before any "rule". This is what worked for Barnie, not professional advice; if your dog stops eating or you're worried about them in the cold, talk to your vet or a qualified behaviourist.
::

**Next steps:** let him rest and decompress on the landing, keep the house quiet and predictable, and let him set the pace for coming back downstairs, one calm evening at a time.
