Over the past week and a half, Barnie has been teaching us what real, self-paced decompression looks like for a Romanian rescue dog.

He hasn't claimed the whole house yet. Instead, he has meticulously established his own sanctuary: the carpeted landing at the top of the stairs.

![Barnie, a cream-coloured rescue dog, curled up in the corner of a carpeted landing, eyes half closed](/images/journal/barnie-first-10-days/curled-up-on-the-landing.webp){width="1440" height="1418" style="max-width:24rem;margin-inline:auto;display:block"}

*His corner of the landing: the spot he has made his own.*

## Why one stretch of carpet matters so much

Spending days anchored to a single stretch of carpet might look like stagnation. It certainly feels that way sometimes! But I have to keep reminding myself that to a dog emerging from 16 months of sensory overload, that landing is an indispensable high-ground fortress.

It gives him total line of sight, a quiet perimeter, and a space where no one demands anything from him. From this safe base, his recovery hasn't been about sudden leaps. It's been about a routine we've come to call **"rubber banding"**.

![Barnie stretched out full length on the landing carpet, resting his head on the floor](/images/journal/barnie-first-10-days/stretched-out-on-the-landing.webp){width="1440" height="748" style="max-width:36rem;margin-inline:auto;display:block"}

*Stretched out full length on his stretch of carpet.*

## Rubber banding: one mealtime at a time

Every mealtime is a calculated exercise in agency.

With the living room door shut to give him a quiet, unobserved corridor, Barnie quietly comes down the stairs, claims his food from the bottom step, and snaps right back up to the security of his landing bed to eat in peace.

Each run down those stairs is a deliberate choice to stretch his comfort zone. And every safe return to his landing reinforces that our home is a place of absolute security, not a trap.

![Barnie lying on the landing beside his food bowl, with his bed mat in front of him](/images/journal/barnie-first-10-days/landing-bed-and-bowl.webp){width="1366" height="1440" style="max-width:24rem;margin-inline:auto;display:block"}

*On the landing, with his bed and bowl close by.*

That fits with what the [first three weeks guide](/guides/decompression-first-three-weeks) says about this stage: routine and predictability are what tell a nervous dog the world is safe.

## The garden: patrols, and a late night or two

Earlier on, Barnie would explore the garden, but it led to the same pattern. After a session of patrolling, he'd anchor himself to one spot outside, and it often took until the early hours to get him back into the house.

I've since learned that the patrolling is likely a perimeter check: he was looking for escape routes in case something bad happened. That's completely understandable when you've been teleported from a rescue pen shared with 15 other dogs to a home in another country!

## Decompression isn't a straight line

Pawprints to Freedom gave us access to an online video series that prepares you for exactly this kind of thing.

Some days it can feel like regression. But decompression isn't linear, and the only real tool we have is patience. Barnie is setting the pace, and for now, the landing is his whole world. That's OK.
