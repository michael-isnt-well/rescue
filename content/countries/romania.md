Romania is where the largest number of overseas rescue dogs coming to the UK originate, and it's the country we're adopting from ourselves — so it's the one we can speak to first-hand. Most dogs come from public shelters or the street, via UK-registered rescues that handle the paperwork and transport.

## Getting a Romanian dog to the UK

Romania is an EU country, so the entry requirements are the standard ones — microchip, rabies vaccination, a 21-day wait, and tapeworm treatment before arrival. See the [rabies and 21-day guide](/guides/rabies-titre-test-timeline) for the timeline. Dogs usually travel by road, a multi-day haul across Europe; read [transport day](/guides/transport-day) for what arrival is like. Because rescue dogs are imported under commercial rules, your dog must be delivered to the address on its paperwork, not collected from a car park or services, and must stay there for at least 48 hours.

## The health tests that matter

::callout{type="warning"}
Disease risk is where country of origin genuinely matters, and it's health information — treat this as general and confirm the right tests with your own vet. Some infections have long incubation periods, so screening on arrival and again months later is often advised.
::

**Brucella canis** is the headline concern for Eastern European dogs: it's considered endemic in Eastern Europe, and ESCCAP UK & Ireland has called for testing of dogs imported from endemic countries to be made compulsory. In a 2023 study of imported dogs published in *Veterinary Record*, 24% carried at least one exotic pathogen, and **Leishmania** was found most often in dogs with Romanian-origin microchips — so a full imported-dog blood panel (Brucella, Leishmania, Ehrlichia, Babesia, heartworm) is worth discussing with your vet.

## The dogs

Romanian rescue dogs are overwhelmingly mixed-breed shelter and street dogs of every size and age, rather than a particular breed type. The DNA-testing company Embark even reports a distinct ancestry category for them, the **Romanian Village Dog**, which it describes as free-ranging, free-breeding dogs descended from the region's founding dog populations rather than from modern breeds.

### Romania's native breeds

Romania is also home to large shepherd breeds, developed over centuries to guard flocks in the Carpathian mountains. If your dog looks like a shepherd type, these are the breeds worth knowing about. What follows is how each breed's official FCI standard describes it, not a prediction about your dog.

| Breed | Standard size (height at shoulder) | Typical look | Bred for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Romanian Carpathian Shepherd** | Males 65–73 cm, females 59–67 cm | Wolf-like head; wolf-grey coat, pale fawn overlaid with black | Defending the herds, and an excellent guard dog |
| **Romanian Mioritic Shepherd** | Males at least 70 cm, females at least 65 cm | Long, abundant coat; white with black or grey patches, or solid white or grey | Herding and guarding; "wary of strangers" |
| **Romanian Bucovina Shepherd** | Males 68–78 cm, females 64–72 cm | White or white-beige with grey or black patches; a mane at the neck | Guarding flocks; "a strong bark", patrolling at night |

A fourth native breed, the **Romanian Raven Shepherd**, is named for its mostly black coat ("corb" means raven in Romanian) and comes from the Carpathian and sub-Carpathian regions.

All four were bred to guard livestock. The FCI standards describe the three recognised breeds as calm and stable, and also as guardians: "a natural guardian" (Carpathian), "wary of strangers" (Mioritic), and a dog whose bark is "very strong" when strangers approach and who patrols at night (Bucovina). That's useful background if you're wondering where watchfulness or night-time barking comes from during the [first three weeks](/guides/decompression-first-three-weeks), but it's never a reason to assume anything about an individual dog.

::callout{type="info"}
You can’t reliably tell a mixed-breed dog’s ancestry by looking at it. In a study of 919 US shelter dogs, staff identified at least one breed in a dog’s DNA in 67.7% of cases, but got more than one breed right in just 10.4%. See [what breed is my rescue dog?](/guides/what-breed-is-my-rescue-dog) for what DNA tests can and can’t tell you.
::
