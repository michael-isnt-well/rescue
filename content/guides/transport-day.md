Transport day is the moment months of waiting finally becomes real. It's also, for your dog, the end of an exhausting journey and the start of everything being unfamiliar at once. A calm, well-prepared arrival makes a real difference to how the first night goes.

## The journey your dog has just had

Most dogs travel by road in a licensed transporter's van, often for **three to four days** across Europe. They travel in crates, with the driver making scheduled stops for water, toileting and rest. It's a legal requirement that these journeys are properly documented and that the vehicle and driver are authorised for animal transport.

By the time the van reaches you, your dog has spent days in motion, surrounded by unfamiliar dogs and people, with no idea where it's going. Whatever personality you've been promised, the dog that steps out of the van is a tired, overwhelmed version of it. That's normal.

::callout{type="warning"}
The single biggest risk on arrival is a dog bolting. A frightened dog can slip a collar in an instant, and a lost overseas rescue in a strange country is a genuine emergency. Use a well-fitted harness **and** a slip lead as backup, and never open the van or your car until you're inside a fully enclosed space.
::

## Door-to-door, or a meeting point?

Many transporters offer **door-to-door delivery**. Where that's available, it's usually the kinder option: it gets your dog out of the van and into its new home with the least additional stress. Some transports use regional drop-off points instead, where you meet the van and take your dog the last leg yourself.

Either way, confirm the plan with your rescue **before** the day, including a rough arrival window and a phone number for the driver. Cross-Europe timings drift, and a van that was due at 2pm can easily arrive at 9pm.

## Getting your dog safely inside

Have a plan for the handover before the van pulls up:

- Do it in an **enclosed space** — a closed room, or a garden with the gate shut and gaps checked — never on an open driveway or street.
- Get the **harness and slip lead** on before the dog has both feet on the ground.
- Keep it quiet: one or two calm people, not the whole family and the neighbours.
- Let the dog come out in its own time. Don't drag it; don't crowd it.

## The first few hours

Once your dog is safely inside, the goal is simple: let it decompress. Offer water. Show it where it can hide — a crate with a blanket over it, or a quiet corner. Then, mostly, leave it be. Resist the urge to introduce it to everyone or to start bonding. That comes later.

What happens over the following days and weeks — the hiding, the not eating, the slow thaw — is its own subject. The first three weeks have a rhythm worth understanding before you're in them.
